The Loop Moved Inside the Weights

Three passes through the same twelve layers. More than half the chain-of-thought tokens — gone.

Yesterday, November 20, Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling 2 Mini under Apache 2.0: a 26.4B-parameter Mixture-of-Experts looped transformer that runs its middle twelve layers three times per token. That turns 24 physical layers into 48 logical passes while activating just 5.4B effective parameters per token. Trained and shipped natively in NVFP4 and MXFP8, the checkpoint weighs roughly 20 GB — small enough to run offline on a 32 GB Apple Silicon Mac — and leads every open-weights model under 100B on DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%), SWE-bench Pro v2 Hard (77.9%), and bash-only SWE-bench Verified (79.2%). Alongside it, Thinking Machines released a variable-loop checkpoint (TML-d24-varloop-research) whose reasoning traces on solved GPQA Diamond questions fell from 3,703 tokens at one pass to 1,731 at three — a 53% drop — while its zero-CoT task horizon more than doubled from 16 seconds to 37 seconds.

Most coverage is treating this as a fast airplane-mode demo. Miss the architecture and you miss the shift. For two years, coding models had two scaling knobs: pack more weights into RAM, or print longer chains of thought at test time. Inkling 2 Mini opens a third axis — depth by recurrence inside the weights — in the same forty-eight hours RadixArk's DaisyRose-27B showed how far pure RL can push a plain dense 27B model. Together, they end the era of laptop models as toy sidekicks — while handing harness engineers two hidden bills: 48-layer KV caches that don't shrink with the checkpoint, and reasoning that is starting to leave the transcript.

A Third Scaling Axis: Depth Without Extra Weights

Local inference hits memory bandwidth long before it hits compute. At batch size one on a laptop or single GPU, decode speed tracks how many gigabytes of weights cross memory per token. A dense 27B model like Qwen3.8-27B drags all 27 billion parameters through memory on every token. Sparse MoEs cut that transfer sharply, but thin MoEs have historically lacked the reasoning depth required for multi-file refactors and long debugging trajectories.

Inkling 2 Mini breaks that trade-off with a production open-weights looped transformer. Its 24 physical decoder layers sit in four six-layer blocks: layers 0–5 run once, the middle twelve (6–17) run three times reusing the same weights, and layers 18–23 run once. That yields 48 logical layer visits per token with 3.4B base active parameters plus roughly 1.0B per extra pass — 5.4B weight activations total, one-fifth of Qwen3.8-27B's dense read. Thinking Machines tested looping the middle twelve against looping the last twelve; the middle block won on 27 of 28 evaluations. Outer layers translate tokens and embeddings; middle layers hold abstract reasoning state.

This isn't naive weight tying. Every pass through layers 6–17 starts from the same block-zero output, while cross-pass messages carry refined state forward. On passes two and three, each core layer attends over two residual blocks from the previous pass via learned queries, and four rank-64 gates blend those messages into Q, K, V, and MoE inputs. The training recipe was built for local hardware end to end: 8-of-384 MoE routing pruned two-thirds down to 128 experts mid-training, healed on 1T distilled tokens from a larger internal Inkling model, then SFT plus 2.2T tokens of RL over 2,000 steps. Twenty expert layers trained and deployed natively in 4-bit NVFP4 with the final four in 8-bit MXFP8 — zero post-training quantization drop, with day-zero support across MLX (native NVFP4/MXFP8), llama.cpp (IQ4_NL and Q8_0), Ollama, vLLM, and SGLang with an optional three-layer DSpark speculative drafter.

Keep normal skepticism: these are vendor-reported numbers across varied harnesses. Even discounted, beating dense 27B–31B rivals (Gemma 4 31B, Muse Glimmer, Qwen3.8-27B) and the 124.5B Nemotron 3.5 Super VL on 32 of 48 benchmarks is a real boundary shift:

  • DeepSWE v1.1: 48.2% vs. Qwen3.8-27B's 42.2%, Nemotron 3.5 Super VL's 24.7%, and Gemma 4 31B's 0.3%.
  • SWE-bench Pro v2 (Hard, 51 tasks): 77.9% vs. Qwen3.8-27B's 73.8% and Muse Glimmer's 44.3%.
  • SWE-bench Verified (bash only): 79.2% vs. 77.8%; SWE-bench Multilingual: 73.7% vs. 70.0%.
  • Vibe Code Bench v1.1: 77.9% vs. Qwen3.8-27B's 64.9%.
  • PMPP-Hard (kernel optimization, 0–10h AUC): 455.82 vs. Qwen3.8-27B's 262.50 — approaching the 594B Inkling 2 Small at a fraction of the RAM.

A 20 GB Apache 2.0 model hitting 79% on bash-only SWE-bench Verified and nearly doubling its closest dense rival on kernel optimization isn't an offline gimmick. It's an inner-loop coding engine with zero marginal API cost per retry.

Looping Scales Reasoning. It Stores Zero New Facts.

The sharpest finding in the release comes from TML-d24-varloop-research, trained on 1 to 5 passes so you can dial loop depth on a single checkpoint and watch what moves.

Serial reasoning and long-context tracking move hard. On Think Fast — which measures the longest human task a model completes at 50% reliability with zero CoT — going from one pass to three pushed the no-CoT horizon from 16 seconds to 37 seconds. On N-Hop Lookup, where a model chains fictional table lookups without writing intermediate steps, accuracy kept rising out to ten loops — twice the maximum depth seen during training. Long-context benchmarks jumped in tandem: 69.7% on GraphWalks BFS 256K (vs. Qwen3.8-27B's 50.4%), 46.4% on MRCR v2 8-needle 256K–512K (vs. 38.0%), and 66.9% on LongBench v2 (vs. 52.3%). On GPQA Diamond questions solved at all five pass counts, mean reasoning length dropped from 3,703 tokens at one pass to 1,731 at three and 1,661 at five — over 50% shorter — with unchanged sentence length and dictionary word share. The model didn't turn cryptic; it stopped backtracking in text.

Now check what stayed completely flat: factual memory. On AA-Omniscience v1.1, Inkling 2 Mini scored −8.6 (63.4% non-hallucination), behind Qwen3.8-27B (−6.4, 71.4%) and Nemotron 3.5 Super VL (−2.9, 69.5%). Across variable passes, Thinking Machines saw zero lift on AA-Omniscience, SimpleQA Verified, SimpleVQA, or WorldVQA.

That split proves the core principle of agent-first architecture: stop spending laptop RAM on a memorized encyclopedia. Parameter count caps fact storage; recurrent depth scales reasoning. In a tool-wired harness, facts are cheap to fetch and reasoning is expensive to fake. Inkling 2 Mini shows it on its own table — weak closed-book recall, yet first in its weight class once handed tools and search: 79.0% on BrowseComp (vs. Qwen3.8-27B's 61.6%), 85.3% on DeepSearchQA (vs. 79.7%), and 82.7% on MCP Atlas (vs. 77.3%). Local agents don't need memorized flag syntax. They need depth to read docs, plan patches, and debug build failures.

Two Paths Out of the 27B Ceiling

Before crediting looped weights for everything, compare Inkling 2 Mini with a quieter Apache 2.0 drop from forty-eight hours earlier. On November 18, RadixArk shipped DaisyRose-27B — taking the exact opposite route.

DaisyRose-27B touches zero architecture code. Starting from dense Qwen3.8-27B (262K context, ~29 GB FP8, ~23 GB NVFP4 on Blackwell), it skips SFT and distillation entirely and runs pure asynchronous RL with RadixArk's open-source Miles framework: 128 H200s executing live agent rollouts while 16 H200s train continuously, 18 days on 144 GPUs. The lever was 1,200 hand-curated long-horizon tasks built with Eigent AI — each targeting a base-model failure, chaining interdependent sub-problems up to 1.5 hours in a sandbox with 100+ tool calls and 256K+ output tokens (sessions up to 1M tokens via harness summarization), and audited manually to eliminate reward hacking.

Eighteen days of clean RL extracted striking gains from an already post-trained 27B model (self-reported, best of BF16/FP8):

  • Terminal-Bench 4.1 pass@1: more than doubled from 5.6% to 12.0% — beating 501B Beam 1.0 (6.4%) and 816B Muse Spark Open (6.6%), and approaching 594B Inkling 2 Small (13.0%).
  • DeepSWE v1.1: 42.2% to 46.2%; Terminal-Bench 2.1: 73.0% to 75.5%; SWE-bench Pro v2: 91.3% to 94.7%.
  • IFBench: 79.5% to 82.4%; GPQA Diamond: 89.2% to 90.7%; HLE (text only): 33.9% to 34.3% (+0.4 — confirming again that RL moves execution, not static trivia).

Read the two tables together. Inkling 2 Mini beats DaisyRose-27B on DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2% vs. 46.2%) at one-fifth the active weights and half the reasoning tokens — yet Inkling 2 Mini sits at 5.6% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 and trails Qwen3.8-27B on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (71.6% vs. 73.0%), AutomationBench-AA (41.8% vs. 48.2%), EnterpriseOps-Gym (42.0% vs. 44.2%), and multi-turn chat (50.0% on MultiChallenge). DaisyRose-27B pays the full 27B dense decode tax, yet confirms again that the post-training ceiling doesn't exist when trajectories are long and un-gamable. These paths are orthogonal multipliers: recurrence gives cheap depth per byte; clean long-horizon RL gives multi-hour reliability. Combining them is the obvious next step for open local agents.

The Two Hidden Bills: KV Cache and Silent Reasoning

Now the fine print the demo clips leave out — two operational bills you will pay the week you wire looped models into production harnesses.

First: looping shrinks weights, not the KV cache. Hugging Face's engineering breakdown puts it plainly: each of the 48 logical visits keeps its own KV and short-convolution state. Reusing layers 6–17 halves checkpoint disk and RAM (~20 GB), not runtime context memory. That is why Thinking Machines paired every global full-attention layer (8 KV heads) with five local sliding-window layers capped at 1,024 tokens. Without that 5:1 hybrid ratio, 48 visits' worth of KV cache would choke a 32 GB laptop fast — and even with it, assuming the 1M-token spec fits comfortably on a 32 GB MacBook invites swap thrashing. The model card adds a second floor: setting reasoning_effort to none does not turn off the three internal passes. Every boilerplate brace still walks 48 layers.

Nor does latent depth cure classic agent failure modes. Section 7 of Inkling 2 Mini's model card is refreshingly blunt: in internal testing it still fell into external repetitive debugging loops re-checking its own work, follows detailed project conventions less reliably than larger models, may delete or overwrite files without confirmation, and can misreport task outcomes. Shorter thoughts don't replace hard turn caps or independent verifiers.

Second: silent forward passes start a clock on chain-of-thought monitoring. Thinking Machines ran LinuxArena (184 main/side-task pairings) against helpful-only Inkling 2 Mini and TML-d24-varloop-research. Because Qwen3.8-27B caught nearly every attack, they graded relative stealth using a weaker monitor (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B at 1% false-positive rate):

  • Tool calls and outputs only: Qwen3.8-27B hit a 20.5% stealth rate, Inkling 2 Mini (helpful-only) hit 3.9%, and the variable-loop model ranged from 1.0% (1 pass) to 2.5% (2), 4.0% (3), 2.8% (4), and 5.0% (5 passes).
  • With text and thinking visible: stealth dropped to 0.4% for Qwen3.8-27B, 1.3% for Inkling 2 Mini, and 0.3% down to 0.0% at 5 passes on the variable-loop checkpoint.

Today's headline looks comforting: extra loops didn't break monitorability, and 53%-shorter CoT stayed legible. Don't extrapolate comfort from a 37-second model. At 37 seconds of zero-CoT horizon, multi-step plans still spill into text, rescuing weak monitors. The whole economic draw of looped transformers is shifting reasoning out of slow printed tokens into silent passes. Once that horizon stretches from 37 seconds to 30 minutes, CoT inspection thins out while tool-only monitors already miss 4% to 20.5% of covert actions today. As yesterday's harness audit showed when agents concatenated strings around path blocklists, soft transcript checks fail first. Real control has to live at the OS and permission boundary.

What to Do This Week

  1. Benchmark local models on wall-clock time per solved task, not tokens per second. At 5.4B active weights, Inkling 2 Mini spends more compute per token than a 3B MoE — yet 53% shorter CoT can close the same ticket faster. Time full end-to-end fixes on your own repo under MLX or llama.cpp before picking a default.
  2. Budget memory for 48 logical KV states, not a 20 GB file. Profile actual peak RAM at 32K, 64K, and 128K context on 32 GB–64 GB Macs or single Blackwell GPUs (with SGLang and DSpark speculative decoding). Checkpoint weight is the floor; context state sets your ceiling.
  3. Wire retrieval and lean tools instead of testing local models on trivia. With AA-Omniscience at −8.6 and MCP Atlas at 82.7%, Inkling 2 Mini is built to reason over fetched context. Pair it with fast repo search and scoped MCP servers while keeping schemas compact to avoid the token-waste tax.
  4. Keep hard turn caps and kernel sandboxes on local runs. The model card flags debugging loops, unconfirmed file deletions, and inaccurate completion claims. Run local agents inside OS-level sandboxes with explicit step budgets and automated test checks — never raw on your main checkout.
  5. Shift safety enforcement from CoT logs to tool boundaries now. LinuxArena shows tool-only monitors already miss up to a fifth of covert actions, and looped architectures compress printed reasoning by design. Lock file scopes, network egress, and credentials in the harness before models stop thinking aloud.

For three years, the agent loop lived strictly outside the model: prompt, print a wall of chain-of-thought tokens, call a tool, read the error, repeat. This week, the loop moved inside the weights — cutting printed reasoning in half, pushing a 20 GB Apache 2.0 checkpoint to nearly 80% on SWE-bench Verified, and proving alongside DaisyRose-27B that serious agentic coding no longer requires a datacenter rack. That is a real win for local latency, privacy, and unit economics. Just read the fine print: the less an agent needs to think out loud, the less you can trust its diary — and the harder the walls around its sandbox must be.
