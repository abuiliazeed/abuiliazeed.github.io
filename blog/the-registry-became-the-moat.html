The Registry Became the Moat

Fifty million.

That's how many agents organizations have registered in Microsoft's Agent 365 since the platform launched in May, according to the number Microsoft put at the top of its Ignite announcements this week. Averaged over six months, it's a shade over a quarter-million new registered agents every day. The same forty-eight hours also produced Gemini 4 Neon, a frontier model at $3 per million input tokens that ranks third overall on the Artificial Analysis intelligence index. Guess which number decides more about how software gets built over the next five years.

Here's the uncomfortable answer for anyone who spends their week tracking model releases: it isn't the benchmark. A model at commodity prices is, by definition, replaceable — that's what the price is telling you. A registry your entire agent estate is enrolled in, with identity, risk scoring, spend tracking, and discovery that reaches onto employee laptops, is the opposite of replaceable. This week the model layer got dramatically cheaper and the control plane got dramatically stickier, in the same news cycle, and most coverage only noticed the first part.

What Fifty Million Registered Agents Actually Means

First, the honest read. It's a vendor-reported number, "registered" is inventory rather than active daily use, and nobody outside Microsoft can audit what's inside the count. Apply it the same discipline you'd apply to a vendor benchmark — which is to say, take the shape seriously even if you doubt the decimals.

What the shape says: six months ago, "put your agents in a governed corporate registry" was a Gartner-slide idea. This week it was something organizations had done fifty million times. Microsoft's own framing of what that scale brings is the tell: who owns each agent, what risk do they carry, and what do they cost — with a new Business Analytics surface in preview for usage, spend forecasting, and recommendations. Read that back. Agents became a budget line with a dashboard before most companies have finished their first serious deployment. Governance is arriving ahead of adoption instead of two years behind it, which is the exact opposite of how every previous technology wave behaved. SaaS needed a decade of sprawl before anyone bought a SaaS management platform. Agents got one at launch.

There's a simple reason, and it's the reason this whole story matters: agents act. A rogue spreadsheet sits there. A rogue agent with credentials does things. The cost of not having an inventory stops being theoretical the first time one of those fifty million entries misbehaves.

Rivals Just Enrolled Their Agents in Microsoft's Directory

The quieter announcement at Ignite is the strategically wild one. Entra Agent ID — the mechanism that gives an agent its own identity inside a customer's Microsoft tenant — previously covered Amazon Bedrock agents. This week it expanded to agents from Google, Salesforce, Databricks, Snowflake, Anthropic, and OpenAI, with a guided connection wizard and secretless authentication so no keys sit in config files (preview, with capabilities varying by platform).

Sit with why that sentence exists at all. Anthropic and OpenAI are fighting Microsoft for the same customers. Their agents now get first-class identities in Microsoft's directory, enrolled through Microsoft's wizard, governed by policies Microsoft's admin tools enforce. They agreed to this — because the customer's identity provider is where the customer's policy lives, and an agent without an identity in the tenant is, by construction, the ungoverned one. The vendors didn't concede the model war; they conceded that agents follow the org, not the vendor.

If this rhyme sounds familiar, it should. Nobody loved Active Directory; every software vendor on earth supported it anyway, because that's where the users were. Identity systems win by being boring and mandatory. Six weeks ago, identity, budgets, and kill switches for agents shipped as primitives from three vendors in a single day. The open question then was adoption. This week it got an answer, at fifty-million scale.

The Laptop Became the Perimeter

Now the part that should genuinely reorder your priorities. The same Ignite wave includes local agent discovery: Microsoft Defender and Entra Global Secure Access can now discover more than sixty kinds of local AI agents — Claude Code and GitHub Copilot CLI are the named examples — and the MCP servers they're configured to talk to. Defender discovery is generally available on managed Windows and macOS devices, tying each agent to its device and user; Global Secure Access adds network-level visibility into each agent's destinations, MCP servers, and resources; and discovered "shadow" agents flow into the same Agent 365 registry as everything else (that last piece is in preview).

The MCP detail is the load-bearing one. Visibility into "an agent binary exists on this laptop" is endpoint management from 2005. Visibility into which untrusted servers the agent is wired to is new, and it's the thing that actually matters — we've spent a year learning that the protocol layer trusted every server and that an instruction file can become an exploit. Enterprises couldn't govern what they couldn't enumerate. Now the enumeration ships with the endpoint agent.

There's an irony worth naming: the coding tools marketed hardest on running locally, on your machine, under your control, are now the most discoverable agents in the estate. That's not a scandal — it's a design reality, and frankly a healthy one. The granularity is what makes it survivable: the new controls can restrict a risky agent while preserving the employee's own access, which kills the old blunt instrument of blocking the human to block the tool. But if you build agents, the message is unambiguous. If it runs on a corporate endpoint, it will be discovered, catalogued, and risk-scored. Build the version of your agent that survives that.

Governance Shipped as Code the Same Day

A day after Ignite, the developer-native version of the same principle arrived. Pulumi launched Pulumi Agents — agents that handle incident investigation, planning, and infrastructure maintenance — and the notable part isn't what they do, it's how they're defined: in code, in source control, with each agent carrying its own identity and Pulumi Cloud role independent of whoever created it. Access control deliberately separates permission to create or edit an agent from permission to participate in its tasks, and every request inside a task must pass both the human's permissions and the agent's role. Triggers come from Datadog alerts, Honeycomb, PagerDuty incidents, webhooks, and Pulumi's own drift and failed-update events. In delivery pipelines, an agent can assess proposed changes against your risk criteria and a rule can require human approval before anything high-risk deploys.

That is the same architecture the Ignite wave pushes enterprise-wide — identity per agent, dual permission checks, risk gates with humans at the edges — expressed as a spec in version control instead of a policy console. And it wasn't alone. Amazon OpenSearch Memory reached general availability the same day, which means even memory, the substrate agents carry between sessions, now ships as a governed managed service: IAM-scoped stores, actor and session scoping, expiration policies on events and memories, with extraction, consolidation, and retrieval handled serverlessly across five regions.

Line up the seventy-two hours: identity, discovery, risk scoring, memory, and spend each picked up a policy surface — from Microsoft, Pulumi, and AWS, plus Azure Monitor previews I'll get to. Even Google's own Stitch Loop, a long-running product agent wired into codebases and live metrics, gates every change behind a human-approved pull request. Nobody is shipping autonomy without a ledger anymore. The interesting vendors stopped arguing about whether agents should be governed and started competing on who governs them best.

You Can't Govern What You Can't Trace

The final piece is observability, and it's the one technologists should care about most. Azure Monitor's new unified agent observability (preview) traces a single request across multiple agents, platforms, models, and tools, using OpenTelemetry trace context and the GenAI semantic conventions. A support question that bounced from a voice agent to a RAG lookup to a tool call to another model's judgment becomes one trace, with spans attributed to the component that spent the latency.

This matters because a registry without telemetry is a phone book, and a risk score without traces is astrology. Discovery tells you an agent exists; traces tell you what it did, and the distance between those two is where every real incident investigation lives. The neutral standard is the quiet win here: OpenTelemetry isn't Microsoft's, which means the same instrumentation works for Bedrock agents, local CLIs, and whatever you built yourself. Pair it with the dynamic agent-risk view now in preview — one continuously updated risk number following an agent across the admin center, Defender, Entra, and Purview — and the control plane stops being a static list and becomes a live instrument panel.

Why This Is a Moat — and Where It Pinches

Strategically, this is the most defensible thing any vendor has built in the agent era, and it's worth being precise about why. Models commoditize — that's literally what Gemini 4 Neon's $3 price tag announced in the same news cycle means. Registries compound. Every agent enrolled, risk-scored, traced, and budgeted inside a tenant raises the cost of leaving: you're not switching a model, you're re-papering an estate — identities, permission histories, risk baselines, dashboards your CFO already reads. The moat moved again, and it moved away from the weights one more time.

The pinches are real too, and an honest analyst counts them. Much of this is preview, not default-on: third-party Entra identities vary by platform, dynamic risk and the shadow-agent registry are previews, multi-tenant management is in public preview, and "Fifty million" is unaudited self-reporting. Previews get delayed; some get cancelled. And a control plane this sticky is exactly as benevolent as the export story behind it — which today is mostly a question nobody is asking, because nobody has fifty million reasons to leave yet. That's precisely when you should ask.

What to Do This Week

  1. Count your agents before someone else does. Inventory the agent CLIs and MCP servers on your team's machines — today, yourself. If Defender can recognize sixty-plus kinds of local agents, your estate is more visible than your wiki suggests. Discovering yours first turns an audit into a decision.
  2. One identity per agent, forever. No agent holds a human's credentials. Issue scoped identities, record what each can touch, and adopt the dual check Pulumi models: a request passes only if the human's permissions and the agent's role both allow it.
  3. Write the kill criteria before the incident. What risk level triggers containment, who decides, and what the agent does when nobody answers. This is a page of policy now or a week of incident response later.
  4. Turn on GenAI tracing in your own stack now. OpenTelemetry's semantic conventions are vendor-neutral and free to adopt while your estate is small. Retrofitting traces into fifty running agents is how governance projects die.
  5. Make export a selection criterion. Before enrolling anything in a registry — Microsoft's or anyone's — ask what leaves with you: identities, histories, traces, budgets. "We'll discuss it" is an answer; write down that they gave it.

None of this is as exciting as a frontier model, which is exactly the point. The argument throughout Harness Engineering: The Complete Guide to Agent-First Software Development is that outcomes live in the harness — context, tools, verification, recovery — not the weights. This week added a wall to that room: the estate your agents inhabit, with its registry, risk scores, traces, and budget lines. The teams that treat that estate as first-class engineering, rather than something IT does to them later, are the ones whose agents will still be enrolled and running in 2028 — on their terms.

The model war gets a new leader every month. The registry war gets won once. This week, someone took the lead.
