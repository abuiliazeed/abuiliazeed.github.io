Zapier Grades the Flagships Now

Google’s strongest model ever went generally available yesterday, and the first proof in the announcement was not an IQ test. Gemini 4 Carbon leads, per Google, on CursorBench 4.0, Perplexity’s WANDR, and Zapier’s AutomationBench — three benchmarks owned by a code editor, a search company, and an automation vendor. The reference measurements for “frontier intelligence” are no longer published by universities or by the labs themselves. They live inside other companies’ products.

The same day, Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 and published a comparison table in which it loses to Google on Zapier’s benchmark — 51.4 percent to Carbon’s 53.9 — while leading on its own agentic coding suites. Two rival labs, one release day, and both now structure their evidence around product-company harnesses. Most coverage read this week as another model-launch arms race. The more consequential story is who keeps score now, because the scoreboard has owners, and the owners are not neutral.

What Actually Shipped

Strip the demos and Carbon is a specific bet: frontier positioning defined by long-horizon agentic work and cybersecurity, sold at mid-tier prices on a timer. The facts that matter:

Pricing as a forcing function. Carbon launched at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output — the same introductory price as Gemini 4 Argon — and that price expires on November 30, 2026, after which it doubles to $4/$20. A twenty-day evaluation window baked into the list price is a new mechanic. Google is explicitly paying teams to benchmark the model in their own loops before deciding whether to commit.

Cost per task as a launch claim. Google’s headline benchmark sentence doesn’t just claim #1 rankings — it claims them “at more than 2X lower cost per task compared to the next best external model.” Read that carefully: cost per task is a property of a loop, not a model. It depends on how many steps your harness takes, how it manages context, when it stops. A model vendor now advertises a harness metric as a first-class spec. The vocabulary of models and the vocabulary of harnesses have merged.

Customer evals as evidence. Beyond the three product benchmarks, Carbon’s announcement leans on evaluations Google didn’t build: it matched the best model tested on Hebbia’s end-to-end financial research evaluation, ranked first out of 23 frontier model configurations on Balyasny Asset Management’s General Financial Intelligence benchmark, and sits at #2 on the new Multimodal Knowledge Agent benchmark — which Google co-launched with Artificial Analysis — at more than 6X lower average cost per task than the top scorer. When the proof points are named after hedge funds and document platforms, evaluation has become a procurement channel.

Agentic by construction. Carbon is described as using more tokens than Argon to “explore thoroughly, reason deeply, and run extended verification loops” — an admission that its edge is bought with loop length, which is exactly the variable ServiceNow found drove cost more than token price did. It was also the engine behind DeepMind’s AlphaProof Code run that formally verified core security properties of Signal’s implementation with over six million lines of machine-checked Lean proofs in about four weeks. And Google is previewing /swarm in Antigravity: one prompt becomes a coordinated agent team with assigned roles — architectural design, coordination, auditing — paired with lighter models and adversarial verifiers across isolated workspaces. One showcase run turned seven public planning PDFs into a 906,000-triangle interactive 3D reconstruction across ten overnight loops.

Anthropic Published a Loss

The Fable 5.2 release is the sharper evidence of the shift, because Anthropic had the option to curate and didn’t take it. The table in its launch materials compares Fable against Fable 5.1, Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Astra, and Gemini 4 Carbon. Fable wins almost everything: 49.1 percent on FrontierCode 2.0 versus Opus 5.5’s 45.5 and Carbon’s 41.1; 69.9 on FrontierSWE v2 versus Carbon’s 61.2; a Remote Labor Index of 41.3 that doubles Opus 5.5’s 21.3. The prerelease stunts are the usual escalation — a thousand Fable agents formalized Fermat’s Last Theorem in Lean in thirty hours; a single run coordinated over a thousand subagents for eight days and produced an operating system that boots.

But there in the same table: AutomationBench, Carbon 53.9, Fable 51.4. Anthropic published a benchmark it loses, owned by a company that sells workflow automation, in its own launch materials. All scores are self-reported, and the table’s availability row for Carbon was stale within hours — but the shape of the evidence is the point. Two years ago a launch table contained zero rows a competitor could win. Now the rows a competitor wins are the ones buyers trust most, precisely because the grader isn’t the vendor.

The effort-level economics are just as telling. Anthropic reports that Fable at High effort matches Opus 5.5’s best FrontierCode score at roughly half the cost per task, and that Fable at Low effort matches Opus 5.5’s top score on Humanity’s Last Exam at less than a tenth of the cost. “Which effort setting, at which point in the loop” is a routing decision — and Anthropic is now publishing the routing table for you.

Why the Scoreboard Moved

A month ago I wrote that Gemini 4 Argon launched with merged pull requests instead of scores — that the unit of proof had moved from benchmarks to artifacts. This week the scores came back, and their return answers why they left: the old scores stopped discriminating. When every frontier model posts a 74 on Humanity’s Last Exam, that exam can’t tell a buyer anything. Chat-style evals saturated. What didn’t saturate is completion of real work — and real work only happens inside loops that product companies already run at scale.

Three forces converged. First, agent performance is a system property: the same model scores differently in different harnesses, which is why Meta’s benchmark footnote the same week ran each model “in its best published harness.” A score that only exists model-plus-harness has to be measured in a harness, and Cursor, Zapier, and Perplexity own the biggest ones. Second, distribution: those products are where the tokens actually go, so their benchmarks proxy production traffic better than any lab suite can. Third, procurement: enterprise buyers stopped accepting vendor self-grading, and named-customer evaluations — Hebbia, Balyasny — are what a model looks like inside a real budget with real data.

Notice what this does to the balance of power. The labs used to be the certification bodies of intelligence: they built the tests, published the scores, and the industry read them like credit ratings. That authority has quietly transferred to whoever runs the most consequential loops. The model is becoming a component; the harness is becoming the certification authority. If you build a product that orchestrates agents, you are — whether you’ve noticed or not — accumulating exactly the asset the labs now covet: a scoreboard that other people’s flagships get graded on.

The Owners Are Not Neutral

Before celebrating the death of self-graded homework, sit with the problems of the replacement. A benchmark run inside one company’s harness bakes in that harness’s defaults — its context management, its stopping rules, its tool surfaces. Optimization pressure will train models toward the shapes of the three or four loops that grade them. The product companies have their own roadmaps, their own model ambitions, and no obligation to keep the rubric stable; a benchmark that can be revised by a product team is a benchmark with a release cycle. And scope is narrower than it looks: 53.9 on AutomationBench means the best configuration available still fails most end-to-end business workflows it attempts. The scoreboard moving is a statement about measurement, not about the work being finished.

There’s also a subtle exclusion at work. The new scoreboard is only readable by companies with production loops. An open-weight model can top a public eval and still be invisible to CursorBench, because the measurement lives inside a product it isn’t shipped in. Open evaluation didn’t die; it just stopped being the scoreboard that moves markets.

The Twenty-Day Timer

The price mechanic deserves its own read. Introductory pricing that doubles on a fixed date is not a discount; it’s a deadline. Google is telling every engineering team in the world: you have until November 30 to find out whether Carbon wins inside your loop at $2/$10, and after that the experiment costs twice as much. Some teams will lock in volume commitments during the window. That’s the real purpose — converting evaluation into contracts before the evaluation finishes.

The right response is to take the deadline seriously on your own terms. The ServiceNow result still stands: quality ties across top models while cost per attempt varies by a factor of forty. Any “more than 2X lower cost per task” claim is a claim about a loop. Yours has different lengths, different cache behavior, different stopping rules. The only measurement that decides whether Carbon is cheap for you is the one you run yourself — and Google has effectively offered to subsidize it for three weeks.

What to Do This Week

Run the subsidized experiment. Pick one real workflow — the one with the most daily volume — and run it to completion on Carbon and on your current model this week, before the price doubles. Track cost per completed task, not per token. Two hours of setup, and you’ll know whether the frontier actually moved for your workload or only for Cursor’s.

Read launch tables for the concessions. The losses are the honest rows. Anthropic conceding AutomationBench tells you more about where each model’s ceiling is than any win column, and the effort-level rows tell you where a cheaper setting is good enough. A launch table is a routing policy waiting to be extracted.

Start treating your own telemetry as a benchmark. If the industry’s scoreboard now lives inside product loops, then the loop you run is the only grader whose scores predict your outcomes. Instrument completion rate, cost per completed task, and intervention frequency per workflow, and version them like a benchmark — because the moment your product has real volume, that instrument is strategically identical to what Cursor and Zapier are sitting on. Building that instrument deliberately, rather than reconstructing it from logs after the fact, is most of what harness engineering is; I wrote a whole book on it — Harness Engineering: The Complete Guide to Agent-First Software Development — and this week is the cleanest demonstration yet of why the harness, not the model, is where durable advantage accumulates.

The flagships will keep trading the top spots; Carbon won’t hold them all for long. What won’t revert is where the scores live. When Zapier’s benchmark decides which frontier model enterprises believe in, the harness has become the market’s referee — and the only scoreboard you fully control is the one running inside your own loop.
