The Agent Got a Wallet

Sometime on Friday, a ChatGPT agent completed a credit-card purchase using a card number that had never existed before that second and will never work again. The cardholder approved it with a glance — Face ID — while doing something else. Nobody typed a card number, nobody saw one, and the merchant got paid over rails that have been live for a decade.

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet on November 13, and most of the coverage filed it under "agentic commerce, finally." That framing misses what's actually interesting. The payment was never the hard part — network tokenization has been mainstream since tap-to-pay, and the card industry knows how to move money better than almost any industry on earth. What shipped Friday is the thing harness engineers have been circling for two years: a production-grade answer to "how does a human approve an agent's irreversible action" — deployed on a product OpenAI says reaches 1.2 billion people a week, where the irreversible action is spending their money.

Most people think the blocker for agents buying things was trust, or regulation, or nervous card networks. It wasn't. It was that nobody had designed the approval. This week someone did, and the design deserves to be read the way you'd read a competitor's harness spec — because that's what it is.

What Actually Shipped

The facts, stripped of launch-page language:

  • Built with American Express, Mastercard, and Visa, rolling out in dots — OpenAI's always-on agents — for ChatGPT Pro subscribers 18 and older in the US with eligible US-issued cards. Notably, the wallet is not available in Chat, Work, or standalone Codex threads. The money is attached to the agent, not the assistant.
  • Add a card once, in a secure form — never in the conversation. The help docs say it in bold: do not paste card numbers or verification codes into chat. Secrets stay out of the model's context window by construction, not by policy.
  • Every purchase gets one human tap. Before checkout, the user reviews the merchant, items, total, and selected card, then approves with Face ID or a fingerprint. No spending limits, no pre-approved merchants, no category rules exist yet — OpenAI lists all of those as future work.
  • Each approval mints a per-transaction token with its own card number, security code, and expiry, issued by the payment network. The agent never sees the real card; neither does the merchant. Card rewards and dispute protections carry over.
  • Approval is scoped. Per the terms, your authorization covers "the merchant, items or services, amount, card, recipient, delivery details, and other terms shown to you" — nothing more. If a material detail changes before submission, the approval dies and the agent has to ask again.
  • Every tap is recorded. OpenAI says it may keep the purchase details, the confirmation screen, and the approval itself — each one creating "a clear record of the purchase the user authorized the agent to make."
  • The merchant stays the merchant. OpenAI's terms state it is "not a bank, card issuer, payment network, lender, or merchant." The store keeps the customer relationship, the pricing, the fulfillment, the returns, and the liability that comes with them.

For the other side of the handshake, OpenAI shipped guidance rather than a protocol: claim your business in ChatGPT, upload a product feed with live pricing and availability, configure your CDN or firewall to recognize signed agents using Web Bot Auth, and make sure your risk systems can tell a legitimate agent purchase from fraud. Forter, Riskified, Sardine, and Signifyd on the fraud side and Adyen, Checkout.com, and Stripe on the payments side are already lined up to do that telling.

The Payment Was Never the Hard Part

Strip out the AI language and the rails here are unremarkable — which is precisely the point. When you tap your phone at a terminal, the merchant receives a network token instead of your card number; that standard has been mainstream for over a decade. The networks didn't build something fundamentally new for OpenAI. They extended an existing control surface to a new kind of initiator. Banks already know how to evaluate whether a transaction is legitimate. What the payments stack had no answer for was a non-human party acting on a human's behalf — because deciding, not paying, was the unsolved part.

Read the wallet's documents — announcement, terms, help pages — as an engineer, and you are reading a harness spec pointed at money. Scope-limited approval is a capability grant: each tap authorizes exactly one transaction with exactly the parameters shown, nothing beyond it. Re-approval on material change is a diff gate. The stored confirmation screen is an audit log. The secure card form is secrets hygiene enforced by UI instead of instruction. It's the same pattern I watched Sierra's Poppy protocol encode for the agent-facing web last week — identity separated from authorization, tokens out of model context, ask before irreversible actions — except this one governs a credit line instead of a storefront. The internal checklist of every serious agent harness built in the last two years keeps escaping into the world, one interface at a time.

Approval Fatigue Is Not Hypothetical

Now the part the launch page doesn't address. Every purchase requires one biometric tap. Today, for a Pro subscriber in a US preview, that might be two taps a week. But the stated destination is the opposite of two taps a week — OpenAI expects users to eventually "delegate purchases entirely to their agent by setting clear parameters." Between those two states sits the question that decides whether this product is safe at scale: what does the two-hundredth approval look like?

We already have field data on what happens to permission prompts under repetition, and it's ugly. Research from AppOmni, cited in a security briefing this month, traced more than 1,100 public GitHub files containing live passwords and API keys to approval fatigue around coding agents — developers clicking "Don't Ask Again" in Claude Code until the prompt stopped being a decision and became a reflex. Nobody intended to publish credentials. The prompt just stopped meaning anything. Every permission UI a human sees daily decays toward ritual; the only open question is the half-life.

A Face ID tap feels stronger than a dialog box, and biometrics do prove who approved. They don't prove the approver read the screen. The wallet's real defense isn't the tap — it's the scope boundary around the tap: one transaction, fixed parameters, a forced re-ask on any change, a stored record. That's what limits the blast radius of a reflexive approval to a single purchase you can dispute with your bank, using protections that carry over by design. It is a genuinely good harness. It is also, right now, the only line of defense, because the structural controls — spending limits, merchant allowlists, category rules, cadence caps — are all listed as future work. And the terms already contain the shape of the endgame: review each purchase, "or grant ChatGPT the authority to approve each purchase in advance."

When that second option arrives, approval stops being a prompt and becomes a policy — and policy is exactly where the coding-agent world just spent October. Claude Code shipped three releases in eight days that read like a dress rehearsal: pooled group budgets shared across a team, an admin API reporting spend per developer and per day, and a setting that makes the agent deny an action outright instead of pausing to ask — because a prompt nobody reads is not a control. Money is simply the channel with the least tolerance for learning that lesson late.

The Verification Gap Ships Too

One detail deserves a slow read, because it compresses the entire agent-reliability problem into a single help-page sentence: "Ready to check out means your approval is complete. It does not confirm that the merchant has placed your order."

The agent will report success. The report is not the world. OpenAI's own documentation trains users to treat the agent's word as provisional — check for a merchant confirmation before retrying, because a pending charge and a placed order are different facts, and the agent can only see one of them. Every engineer who has watched an agent declare a task complete against an unchanged filesystem will recognize the shape immediately. What's new is the stakes: a phantom "order placed" now comes with a pending charge attached. The wallet handles the authorization gap well. The truth gap is the user's problem, flagged in the fine print.

Your Checkout Became an Agent Surface

The merchant-side math is being decided by defaults, same as last week. Amazon blocked Meta's Muse agents outright earlier this year — a choice to opt out of a demand channel, priced as brand protection. OpenAI's launch guidance makes the opt-in path concrete: sign your agents, publish clean product data, teach your fraud stack what an agent purchase looks like. Merchants who do it first get the traffic; merchants who don't get declines and cancelled orders — the failure mode OpenAI explicitly warns about, where risk systems flag legitimate agent purchases and silently eat the sale.

And the liability fight I flagged last week is still unallocated. PYMNTS found 93% of merchants believe AI providers should bear the loss when an agent errs; the wallet's terms state the opposite arrangement in plain type — the merchant remains merchant of record, subject to their own refund, return, and dispute rules. Both sides expect the other to hold the risk, and neither has it in writing. The first seven-figure agent-purchase dispute is going to be educational.

What to Do This Week

If you build agents, write the transaction policy before you need it. Spend caps, merchant allowlists, category rules, cadence limits — design them now, while your agent's most irreversible action is still a git push. The wallet proves that per-action approval is a viable launch posture and a terrible end state; the teams that survive delegation are the ones whose approvals became policy early. It's the same irreversible-actions discipline whether the action is a deploy, a database write, or a shopping cart — the core of the harness engineering practice I laid out in Harness Engineering: The Complete Guide to Agent-First Software Development, now with a bank attached.

Stress-test your own approval UX against repetition. Show your permission prompt on attempt three and attempt three thousand to someone who didn't build it. If they can't tell the difference in the attention it earns, you don't have a control — you have a ritual with a biometric garnish. Measure the half-life before your users do.

If you sell things, check your front door. Fetch your own site the way an agent would and see what happens. Ask your payment provider how agent-initiated tokens are labeled in your risk dashboard, because the difference between a decline and a sale is now a configuration decision you've never had to make. And get the product feed accurate — the approval screen is where agent-mediated comparison happens, and a stale price loses there silently, without you ever seeing the customer walk.

Watch the map, not the preview. This launched US-only, Pro-only, dots-only, with the three networks on board — and pointedly not in the EU, where payment regulation moves at its own speed. The constraint on agentic commerce was never model intelligence. It's how fast the authorization machinery around the model can be made trustworthy at repetition.

Network tokens were the easy part; the card industry solved them a decade ago, for humans. What Friday actually shipped is the first mass-market capability grant for software: one human glance, scoped to one transaction, recorded forever. If the approval design holds at the two-hundredth tap, this is the moment agent autonomy got a budget. If it doesn't, it's the moment it got a fraud problem. Either way, the design of permission that still means something when nobody is reading it anymore is now the most commercially important problem in harness engineering — and this week it stopped being hypothetical.
